Run and Tumble

An E. coli bacterium has no nervous system and cannot see where food is. It swims in straight runs, tumbles at random, and compares the nutrient signal it senses now with what it sensed over the last few seconds. When things are getting better, it tumbles less.

The green shading is the nutrient signal (attractant concentration). The simulation models chemical guidance only: the cells do not eat, grow, divide, or use up the nutrient.

How the algorithm works

Each bacterium runs the same short program, and it runs it whatever the environment. Every 0.01 simulated seconds it does four things:

  1. Sense. It reads the attractant concentration c at its own position, and nowhere else. It never sees the field, the slope, other cells, or where the food is.
  2. Compare. It converts c into a signal s, and keeps a slowly adapting reference m: a running average of the signal over the last few seconds. The difference s − m says whether things are better or worse than they were recently.
  3. Set the tumble rate. If things are improving, the tumble rate falls; if they are worsening, it rises. Even at its lowest the rate is above zero, so no run is guaranteed to continue, and even at its highest a tumble is only more likely, never certain.
  4. Move. While running it swims at constant speed with a little random wobble. When a tumble happens it nearly stops and turns by a random angle, chosen without any reference to the nutrient.

These words describe the computation, not an experience. “Improving” means only that s is greater than m.

This is a phenomenological teaching model, not a reconstruction of the molecular pathway. In a real cell, attractant binding to receptor clusters reduces the activity of the kinase CheA, which lowers phosphorylated CheY and so makes the flagellar motors less likely to switch into the clockwise rotation that causes a tumble. Slow methylation of the receptors by CheR and CheB plays the role of the adapting reference m.

    Simplifications.
    
    
      
  • Real bacteria swim in three dimensions; this dish is two-dimensional. The turn-angle distribution is chosen to match the forward persistence of real tumbles (mean cosine about 0.33, typical turn about 66°).
    • 
      
  • The nutrient field is fixed. Nothing is consumed, nothing diffuses, and cells do not grow, divide, or interact. Moving the patch changes the field instantly, which a real dish could not do.
    • 
      
  • The walls reflect cells. This is a simulation convenience; reflections are not tumbles and are not counted as tumbles.
    • 
      
  • Bacteria are drawn about 6× actual size with simplified flagella. A real cell is about 2 µm long.
    • 
      
  • Sensing follows s = ln(1 + c/K) with a single adaptation time constant and a symmetric exponential response, clipped at a floor and ceiling. Real cells respond more strongly to increases than to decreases, adapt more slowly after very large steps, and have noisy receptors. Parameter values are illustrative.
