Pattern separation in a toy dentate gyrus

1. Two entorhinal inputs that share almost everything

Each square is one entorhinal feature, on (1) or off (0). F1–F8 describe the shared context, the garage, and are on for both days. F9 is on only for Monday's row, F10 only for Tuesday's.

Click any square to change a pattern. Every figure below follows.

2. Every granule cell gets its own random synapses

Each of the 100 cells receives one weight from every feature, drawn from {0, 1, 2, 3}. Nothing is learned; this wiring exists before either day happens.

Darker squares are stronger synapses. The outlined rows are the features unique to one day. Click a column to follow that cell.

3. A cell adds up its input, and most of that input is shared

A cell's drive is its weighted sum over the active features, u = Σ wᵢxᵢ. F1–F8 are on both days, so they give the cell the same amount, S, on Monday and on Tuesday. The days differ only by the unique term: u₁ = S + w9 and u₂ = S + w10.

Gemini's key comparison is G3 against G7. G7 has the strongest possible Monday synapse (w9 = 3) but an ordinary shared sum, so it never gets near τ. G3's shared sum already sits 2 below τ, so w9 = 3 tips it over on Monday while w10 = 0 leaves it silent on Tuesday.

The shared features aren't subtracted out. They decide which cells are close enough to threshold for the unique feature to matter.

4. The shared pedestal decides which cells can tell the days apart

All 100 cells, sorted by their shared sum S (grey). The red cap adds the Monday-only input, the blue cap the Tuesday-only input; a faded cap didn't reach τ.

    5. Inhibition draws a line, and only the corner separates

    Plot each cell's Monday drive against its Tuesday drive. Because both include the same S, the cells hug the diagonal: no cell can sit more than 3 off it.

    The threshold τ, set by GABAergic inhibition, cuts the plane into four quadrants. Cells that fire on one day only exist just where the diagonal passes the corner at (τ, τ), in the tinted strips.

    Raise τ and that corner slides out into the sparse tail. Fewer cells fire, and a larger share of those that do are one-day cells.

    6. The output: two sparse codes that share fewer cells

    Reading out all 100 cells gives two binary vectors. Cells active on both days carry the context. Cells active on one day carry the difference, but only in combination with the context that lifted them near threshold.

    7. How much separation? It depends on the yardstick

    Raw cosine says Gemini's inputs are 0.89 alike and the outputs 0.50. But raw cosine depends on how dense a code is.

    Two random patterns with 9 of 10 features on would share 8.1 features on average, a cosine of 0.90. Monday and Tuesday share 8, which is slightly less than chance: with 9 of 10 features on, two patterns cannot be any more different. Two random 4-of-100 codes would share 0.16 cells, a cosine of 0.04, so outputs sharing 2 cells are far above chance.

    Correlation (Pearson r) measures overlap against what the densities predict, and it flips the story for this example: about −0.11 in, +0.48 out.

    The case for raw overlap: what hurts a downstream store such as CA3 is one cue reactivating another memory's cells, and that is overlap among active cells. The case for correcting: making any code sparser lowers raw cosine on its own, even when the sparsening throws information away.

    To see separation that survives both yardsticks, give the input room to vary. “Try a sparser input” in step 1 uses 5 of 10 features with 4 shared.

    What this toy leaves out

    Real perforant-path wiring is sparse: each granule cell samples a fraction of a far larger entorhinal population, rather than every feature.

     

    Inhibition is feedback, so the effective threshold rises with total drive, closer to a k-winners-take-all rule than a fixed τ. The “≈4% active” button mimics that. Synapses here are fixed; mossy cells, adult-born neurons and plasticity are left out.
