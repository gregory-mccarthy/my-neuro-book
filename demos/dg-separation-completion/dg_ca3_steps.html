Part 2 · CA3, step by step

Can CA3 keep similar mornings apart and recall each from a fragment?

Part 1 ended with the dentate gyrus making Monday’s and Tuesday’s patterns less alike: 0.89 at the input, 0.50 in the granule cells. The granule cells send their output to CA3, the region thought to store each experience as a pattern of active cells.

Theories of memory ask CA3 for two things that work against each other. Separation: store similar mornings as different patterns, so that recalling Tuesday does not reactivate Monday. Completion: reactivate a whole stored pattern from part of it.

This toy CA3 stores three mornings: Monday and Tuesday in garage A, from part 1, and Wednesday in a second garage, B.

How can one network do both?

Step 1 of 10 · Wiring

Who connects to whom, and how many

CA3 cells get three kinds of input: ② mossy fibres from granule cells, ③ the perforant path straight from the entorhinal cortex, and ④ recurrent collaterals from other CA3 cells. ① is the dentate gyrus’s input, from part 1.

Convergence: how many sending cells each receiving cell hears from. Divergence: how many receiving cells each sending cell contacts.

The rat shows the same contrast at a far larger scale.

What does this wiring carry on each morning?

Step 2 of 10 · The inputs

Three mornings, two garages

The entorhinal cortex now has 20 inputs. F1–F10 describe garage A, as in part 1; F11–F20 describe a second garage, B.

Each granule cell keeps its 10 synapses from part 1 and has 10 more, for F11–F20, each a roll of a four-sided die. F11–F20 are silent on Monday and Tuesday, so the new synapses add nothing on those mornings.

Where do these granule cells send their output?

Step 3 of 10 · Mossy fibres

Each granule cell contacts 3 CA3 cells, at random

Each granule cell’s mossy fibre has 3 CA3 targets, drawn at random from the 100 when the network was built, like dealing 3 cards from a deck. Nothing about the mornings went into the deal.

Which of these CA3 cells fire?

Step 4 of 10 · Storing a morning

The 10 most strongly driven CA3 cells fire

Inhibition in CA3 lets only the 10 cells with the largest total input fire; the other 90 stay silent.

While a morning is stored, each active mossy-fibre contact adds 100 to its CA3 cell. The perforant path adds far less: 0 to 0.3 for each active input a cell is wired to, plus 1 per synapse learned on an earlier morning.

Recurrent synapses are switched off while a morning is stored.

How is each pattern stored?

Step 5 of 10 · Learning

Cells that fire together get connected

After each morning, two kinds of synapse switch on, from 0 to 1 (Hebb’s rule at its simplest; real synapses strengthen gradually):

  • Recurrent: between every pair of CA3 cells that fired together (10 cells make 90 one-way synapses).
  • Perforant path: from each active input to each active CA3 cell it is wired to.

Learned perforant-path synapses add up: when Tuesday is stored, Monday’s are already in place (this matters in step 10).

What happens when a cue arrives later?

Step 6 of 10 · Recall

A cue arrives, and CA3 settles

Later, a cue arrives through the perforant path (1 per learned synapse, plus the weak random ones) and the mossy fibres, which now add μ per contact instead of 100.

The 10 cells with the largest input fire (t = 0). Then each cell also gets α for every active cell it has a recurrent synapse from, and the 10 with the largest total fire. This repeats until nothing changes.

Monday turns into Wednesday, one input at a time

Holding on to Monday is completion; the jump to Wednesday is separation. The dentate gyrus does neither: its output changes as soon as the input does.

What does the dentate gyrus contribute to storage?

Step 10 of 10 · Without the dentate gyrus

Remove the dentate gyrus, and Tuesday is stored on Monday’s cells

Take the mossy fibres away, while storing and at recall. Tuesday then reaches CA3 only through the perforant path, whose synapses already learned Monday (step 5). Most of Tuesday’s inputs are Monday’s, so Monday’s cells get the largest input.

Theory assigns the mossy fibres exactly this job (Treves & Rolls, 1992). Mice lacking NMDA receptors only in granule cells were impaired at telling two similar contexts apart (McHugh et al., 2007).

What the recordings say

  • CA3 gives distinct cell sets to different rooms, while CA1’s overlap (Leutgeb et al., 2004). With small changes CA3 overlaps more than CA1, with large ones less (Vazdarjanova & Guzowski, 2004).
  • CA3 kept a coherent representation while its dentate inputs were strongly disrupted (Neunuebel & Knierim, 2014).
  • Morph experiments disagree: abrupt switches in one study (Wills et al., 2005), a gradual change with hysteresis in CA3 in another (Leutgeb et al., 2005).

What this toy leaves out

  • 100 CA3 cells, three memories and on/off synapses: no capacity limits and no forgetting.
  • Each recall starts afresh, so there is no hysteresis.
  • Storing and recall are separate modes with different mossy-fibre strengths; real CA3 mixes them.
  • Each granule cell contacts 3 CA3 cells, not 11–15, and any CA3 cell can contact any other.

Where the rat numbers come from

  • Cell counts are for one side of the rat brain: about a million granule cells and about 300,000 CA3 pyramidal cells (Amaral, Ishizuka & Claiborne, 1990; Henze, Wittner & Buzsáki, 2002).
  • Mossy fibres. Each granule cell contacts about 14 CA3 pyramidal cells (11–15 in single-cell reconstructions), plus 40–50 inhibitory interneurons. Each CA3 pyramidal cell receives from at most about 46–50 granule cells (Amaral et al., 1990; Acsády et al., 1998; Henze et al., 2002).
  • Perforant path and recurrent collaterals onto CA3. Up to about 3,750 perforant-path and about 12,000 recurrent synapses per CA3 cell (Amaral et al., 1990, as tabulated by Treves & Rolls, 1992). These count synapses, not sending cells; one cell can make more than one synapse.
  • How often two CA3 cells are connected is disputed. Paired recordings in brain slices found under 1% in rats (Guzman et al., 2016) and about 9% in mice (Sammons et al., 2024). Cutting a slice severs axons, so slice numbers may underestimate the intact brain.
  • Entorhinal input to granule cells is not pinned down as a single number. About 60% of a granule cell’s dendrite lies in the outer two-thirds of the molecular layer, where the perforant path ends (Desmond & Levy, 1982), but how many entorhinal cells converge on one granule cell has not been counted.

Why 100, why 10, and why a tie-breaker?

  • 100 per mossy-fibre contact. Mossy-fibre synapses are among the largest in the brain. In anaesthetized rats, a burst of spikes in a single granule cell reliably made its CA3 targets fire (Henze, Wittner & Buzsáki, 2002). The model represents this power by a number larger than all perforant-path input put together; any such number gives the same result.
  • 10 of 100 cells. Inhibition keeps CA3 activity sparse, though less sparse than in the dentate gyrus. The model fixes the number at 10 so that every stored pattern has the same size. The number matters most relative to how many cells the mossy fibres reach: any places left over are filled through the perforant path, which favours cells that learned an earlier morning.
  • The weak random perforant-path synapses (0 to 0.3) stand for connections that exist before any learning. When mossy fibres reach fewer than 10 cells, these small differences determine which other cells fill the remaining places.
  • The tie-breaker. Each CA3 cell has a fixed, tiny random number (under 0.001) added to its input, a stand-in for small differences in excitability. It only matters when two cells would otherwise have exactly the same input.

Why are recurrent synapses off while storing?

  • The problem. If recurrent synapses were active while Tuesday is stored, the synapses learned on Monday would add input to Monday’s cells, and Tuesday would be stored partly on Monday’s cells.
  • A proposed solution. Acetylcholine, released in the hippocampus during active exploration, suppresses transmission at CA3’s recurrent synapses more than at its incoming fibres. Hasselmo, Schnell & Barkai (1995) proposed that this lets new patterns be stored without interference from old ones.
  • In the model this is all or nothing: recurrent input is off while storing and on at recall. The brain is likely to be less clear-cut.

Why are the mossy fibres weaker at recall?

  • The theory. Treves & Rolls (1992) argued that CA3 needs two kinds of input: a few very strong synapses to set up a new pattern during learning (the mossy fibres), and many modifiable ones to start recall (the perforant path).
  • The model simply uses 100 per contact while storing and μ (4 by default) at recall. Move μ to see what changes: at μ = 0 recall runs on the perforant path alone; at large μ the mossy fibres dominate recall too.
  • In the brain, how much a mossy fibre drives CA3 depends on how its granule cell fires. Single spikes in a granule cell generally failed to make CA3 cells fire, while trains of spikes did (Henze et al., 2002). How this differs between learning and recall is not settled.
