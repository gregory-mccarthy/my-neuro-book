PURPOSE

Build a sequence of ten focused figures that carries the chapter’s argument visually. The opening establishes value as a relation between an outcome and a regulated organism. The experimental middle distinguishes the procedure, the measured result, and the inference. The final figure returns those findings to continuing interaction among body, environment, and neural activity.

The proposed figures are original teaching illustrations. No empirical graph should be reconstructed from memory or invented to resemble published data. Where analytical curves are specified below, they are explicitly labeled teaching constructions.

HOW TO USE THE PACKET

For each new model request, supply:
1. The revised chapter.
2. The shared brief below.
3. The appropriate Gemini or Claude production adapter.
4. One individual figure prompt.
5. If available, one already approved figure as a visual style reference.

Ask for one figure at a time. Keep the first returned image or SVG together with the exact prompt and model’s separate legend. This makes subsequent review concrete: we can compare the intended teaching point with the actual composition, labels, anatomy, and contingencies.

Suggested first production batch: Figures 1, 2, and 3. Figure 1 establishes the naturalistic illustration style. Figures 2 and 3 establish the colors, labeling, and plotting conventions used in Figure 4. The remaining figures can proceed once these conventions are satisfactory.

SHARED BRIEF — APPEND TO EVERY REQUEST

Read the attached revised chapter as the authority for its terminology, audience, and argument. Create only the requested figure. The textbook explains neural function through evolution, bodily regulation, and dynamical control. The figure should make one relationship or experiment easier for an undergraduate to understand.

Use a warm-white background, charcoal text, ample space, and restrained natural colors. Preserve an outcome’s identity across conditions with both a label and a stable visual cue. Encode a change in bodily state separately from a change in the outcome itself. Use neutral animal expressions and credible organisms or anatomy where these are requested.

For the juice series, use juice A in ochre (#B8781A), juice B in blue (#2876A8), baseline/control in charcoal (#333333), and stimulation or inhibition in violet (#8062A7). Also distinguish conditions through labels and line styles. These are illustrative design colors, not claims about the original stimulus assignments.

Keep labels inside the figure short. Put the title and explanatory legend outside the artwork, as separate text. Letter panels A, B, C where useful. Prefer numbered experimental phases and plain connectors without arrowheads. Whenever direction is biologically important, explicitly name the source, target, and relation on the connector; do not leave direction ambiguous.

Distinguish procedure, observation, and the chapter’s interpretation. Label every invented teaching plot “Schematic.” Do not add simulated scatter, invented uncertainty intervals, statistical stars, classifier accuracies, fake activation maps, or calibrated neural value meters. A conceptual relationship must not masquerade as a measured dataset.

Preserve the coexistence of outcome identity and comparable value information. Do not introduce a central executive, a single universal value token, or a compulsory serial pipeline from valuation to choice.

Design for a finished width of about 150–165 mm. Important labels should remain at least approximately 9 pt at that size. If the requested material cannot fit legibly, simplify the composition rather than shrinking the text. The figure should also work as a lecture slide.

GEMINI PRODUCTION ADAPTER

Produce one finished illustration, with the requested panel arrangement and the largest suitable resolution available. Favor convincing bodies, environmental space, apparatus, and anatomical orientation. Include only the short labels explicitly requested. Reserve explanatory prose for a separate legend. Do not improvise quantitative plots. When the brief requests an anatomical asset for later assembly, provide that asset without labels or functional overlays. Return the artwork and a separate brief description of what each panel depicts.

CLAUDE PRODUCTION ADAPTER

Return one complete, self-contained, editable SVG, followed by a separate plain-text legend. Use a viewBox, selectable text with text/tspan elements, and clearly named panel groups. Use native SVG shapes and paths. Do not use foreignObject, JavaScript, external fonts, or external image dependencies. Keep text editable rather than converting it to outlines.

For a supplied illustration asset only, an embedded raster image is permitted; all annotations, connectors, and plots must remain vector elements. Otherwise make the figure entirely vector-based. A practical canvas width is 1500 viewBox units; at that scale, important label text generally needs about 32 units to remain readable at 150 mm printed width.

Construct specified curves from their mathematical functions. Inspect the completed render for clipping, overlap, tiny labels, inconsistent colors, and ambiguous connections. Return a finished figure rather than a description of how one might be made. Supply the explanatory caption outside the SVG.

FIGURE 1 — ONE BODY, DIFFERENT NEEDS, DIFFERENT CHOICES

Teaching purpose
Establish the chapter’s starting point before students interpret neural “value” signals: the same opportunities can matter differently to the same body in different conditions.

Gemini prompt
Create a naturalistic, two-panel textbook illustration of the same small field mouse in the same environment. Use a landscape composition, approximately 2:1, with ample warm-white space around the scenes.

In both panels, show a shelter opening at the lower center, a short route toward accessible food under low vegetation on the left, and a different route toward shallow water on the right. Keep the viewpoint, shelter, food, water, vegetation, distances, route costs, and animal identity matched. Food and water must remain recognizable as different outcomes. Make the two initial routes visibly incompatible movements from the same starting place.

Panel A is labeled “Food needed.” Show the mouse beginning to orient and move toward food. Panel B is labeled “Water needed.” Show the same mouse beginning to orient and move toward water. Bodily condition is supplied by these labels, not by exaggerated emaciation, drooling, distress, or facial expression. The animal has adequate water in A and has recently eaten in B; this contextual detail belongs in the separate legend.

Use body orientation and the beginning of a path to show action. Keep both options available in both scenes. Include only the panel letters and the two short state labels. Do not add numerical values, coins, balances, colored brain centers, thought bubbles, or a hidden decision maker.

This is an ecological teaching example, not a reconstruction of a particular behavioral experiment. Its meaning is that the relation between an expected outcome and bodily condition changes. Preserve the richness of the environment while making that comparison immediately visible.

Draft caption
The same available outcomes can support different actions as bodily condition changes. In this illustrative scene, a mouse needing food begins one route; a mouse needing water begins another. The environment and the identities of the outcomes are held constant. What changes is the organism’s relation to the consequences of obtaining them. The illustration expresses the chapter’s starting argument; it does not supply a numerical value scale or a measured prediction about this species.

Source note
Conceptual synthesis of the chapter’s opening. No new empirical result is asserted. The chapter discusses alliesthesia with @cabanac1971pleasure.

First review question
Are the environments actually matched, so that the changed action is not explained by a newly appearing obstacle or a moved reward?

FIGURE 2 — INFERRING AN EXCHANGE RATE FROM REPEATED CHOICES

Teaching purpose
Make the behavioral inference understandable before interpreting the OFC responses.

Claude prompt
Create one SVG with two spacious panels. Panel A explains a monkey’s juice-choice task; Panel B shows an original schematic choice curve. Use the chapter’s juice colors consistently: A ochre, B blue, always accompanied by the letters.

In Panel A, draw four small monitor states in numbered order: central fixation; simultaneous offers; appearance of peripheral choice targets; saccade to one target, followed by juice delivery. Show the offers as colored-square arrays. Use one A square and three B squares for the illustrated offer pair, with central fixation between the arrays. State “Color: juice; number: amount.” In this 2006 task, appearance of the peripheral targets is the go signal. Show delivery outside the screen, after the eye-movement report. Add a small reversed offer display labeled “Left/right positions counterbalanced.” A and B can exchange screen positions without exchanging identity.

In Panel B, plot probability of choosing B on the vertical axis, from 0 to 1, against offered quantity ratio r = qB/qA on a logarithmic horizontal axis. Use ratio ticks 1/3, 1, 3, 9, and 27. Construct the curve exactly as:
P(B) = 1 / (1 + exp[-4 ln(r/3)]).
This is an original teaching construction, not a fit to published data. Use guides through P(B) = 0.5 and r = 3. Label the crossing “Illustrative equivalence: 1A ≈ 3B.” Mark the graph “Schematic.”

Keep the behavioral equivalence prominent. Do not show juice cups as the visual offers, a hand reaching response, observed scatter, error bars, or one animal’s equivalence as a universal exchange rate. The equation is an implementation instruction and need not appear in the artwork.

Draft caption
An exchange rate is inferred from repeated choices. Colored-square arrays specify the identity and amount of each juice; the monkey reports a choice by an eye movement after peripheral targets appear. Offer positions vary across trials. In the schematic curve, either offer is chosen equally often when one unit of A is offered against three units of B. This illustrates equivalence under particular conditions. The curve and its numerical example are teaching constructions, not published observations; quantity ratios are plotted on a logarithmic axis.

FIGURE 3 — DIFFERENT OFC RESPONSES PRESERVE DIFFERENT INFORMATION

Teaching purpose
Separate information about an offered good, the chosen value, and the chosen identity while showing their coexistence.

Claude prompt
Create a spacious 2×2 SVG of four schematic response plots. Each plot represents a different example neuron. The four plots illustrate three functional response classes, because the offer-value class includes A-related and B-related examples. Use the same juice colors and labels as Figure 2.

Panel A, “Offer value A”: plot a response that changes as the offered amount of A increases. Superimpose two differently patterned lines representing different amounts of B; they follow the same A-dependent relation. Both goods are offered on these trials.

Panel B, “Offer value B”: reverse the logic. Response changes with the offered amount of B while example conditions with different amounts of A follow the same B-dependent relation. Use pattern, not juice color, to distinguish the accompanying amounts of the other good.

Panel C, “Chosen value”: show A-choice and B-choice markers lying on the same rising relation at equivalent chosen values. Use three horizontal positions labeled “1A or 3B,” “2A or 6B,” and “3A or 9B.” These use Figure 2’s illustrative exchange rate.

Panel D, “Chosen identity”: use the same three equivalent-value positions, but show one response level for A choices and a different response level for B choices. Keep the two levels approximately flat across the illustrated values.

Label each vertical axis “Example neuron response” with qualitative low/high indicators and add “Independent arbitrary scale.” Avoid numerical firing rates. Label horizontal axes precisely; include a key distinguishing A-choice and B-choice markers. Use “Schematic functional response classes” once across the figure.

These are representative response relationships, not genetically defined cell types or separate anatomical modules. Do not connect the panels into a processing sequence. Do not imply that equal response heights in different neurons establish equal value, or that positive tuning is the only possible tuning direction.

Draft caption
Different responses retain different information about a choice. Offer-value examples vary with one offered good while the other is also available. A chosen-value example follows behaviorally equivalent outcomes across identities. A chosen-identity example distinguishes which juice was selected. The plots use independent arbitrary response scales and an illustrative 1A ≈ 3B exchange rate. They depict functional response classes, not distinct anatomical cell types or a mandatory sequence of computations.

Source and chapter citation
Padoa-Schioppa and Assad (2006), @padoaschioppaassad2006.
https://www.nature.com/articles/nature04676
See the original supplement for response-variable definitions and analyses.

First review question
Can a student identify what varies, what is held comparable, and whether the response concerns the offer or the choice in each panel?

FIGURE 4 — CHANGING RELATIVE VALUATION AND DISRUPTING COMPARISON

Teaching purpose
Make the causal distinction between a displaced exchange rate and less consistent choice visible.

Claude prompt
Create two matched SVG psychometric plots using Figure 2’s axis convention. Both plot probability of choosing B against offered quantity ratio r = qB/qA on a logarithmic axis with ticks 1/3, 1, 3, 9, and 27. Use charcoal solid lines for baseline and violet dashed lines for the altered condition.

Panel A is “Relative valuation changes.” Plot:
Baseline: P(B) = 1 / (1 + exp[-4 ln(r/3)]).
Shifted example: P(B) = 1 / (1 + exp[-4 ln(r/4)]).
Mark the two 0.5 crossings. State “More B needed to offset A” beside the illustrative rightward shift.

Panel B is “Comparison becomes less consistent.” Plot the same baseline and:
Less consistent example: P(B) = 1 / (1 + exp[-2 ln(r/3)]).
Mark the shared midpoint at 3 and make the shallower slope clear. Use the empirical interpretation “No systematic relative-value shift,” rather than claiming that a measured midpoint was exactly unchanged.

Above Panel B, include a compact four-stage sequence: offer 1 at screen center; interval; offer 2 at screen center; later side-target choice report. Mark weak OFC stimulation during offer 2. State that offer order and response positions are counterbalanced. The inset concerns the later sequential-offer experiment, not the 2006 simultaneous-offer procedure.

Add “Different experimental conditions; curves schematic.” These panels summarize distinguishable perturbation effects, not a single continuous dose-response experiment. The direction and size of Panel A’s shift are illustrative; do not imply that stimulation always favors A. Do not invent raw trials, significance tests, or dose labels. Keep the formulas outside the rendered figure.

Draft caption
A midpoint shift and a change in steepness have different meanings. In A, the illustrative shift means that more B is needed to offset A. In B, the less steep curve describes less consistent choice near equivalence. The latter pattern explains the inference from weak OFC stimulation during the second of two sequential offers: comparison was disrupted without a systematic shift in relative value. The panels summarize different experimental conditions; all curves are analytical teaching constructions, and the numerical displacement in A is illustrative.

FIGURE 5 — SHARED VALUE INFORMATION WITH OUTCOME IDENTITY PRESERVED

Teaching purpose

Claude prompt
Create one SVG with two main panels titled “Regional relationships” and “Pattern generalization.” This is a conceptual explanation of human fMRI findings, not a reconstruction of a particular choice display or an activation map.

In Panel A, show three compact category rows: Food, Consumer items, and Monetary outcomes. Each has a recognizable simple icon and a small schematic rising relationship between subjective value and mean BOLD response. Label the axes; use different slopes or intercepts so the drawings do not imply one calibrated BOLD scale. Bracket the rows with “Overlapping vmPFC region.”

In Panel B, show “Train on food fMRI patterns” with distinct low-value and high-value examples. State “Cross-category value information in mPFC.” Low and high refer to participants’ valuations, not an intrinsic value assigned to an object.

At the bottom, add a compact complementary statement: “Category-dependent value patterns also occur in medial OFC.” Keep the food and object icons visible there. Shared value information does not require identical complete activity patterns or loss of category identity.

If a checked anatomical asset is supplied, use a small orientation inset with medial and ventral human brain views. Add broad vmPFC and OFC callouts without sharp functional borders; omit the inset if it forces the explanatory panels below readable size. Keep those callouts separate from the result diagrams.

FIGURE 10 — CHOICE CHANGES THE BODY THAT CHANGES THE NEXT CHOICE

Teaching purpose
Return the experimental findings to the author’s account of recurrent, embodied control.
